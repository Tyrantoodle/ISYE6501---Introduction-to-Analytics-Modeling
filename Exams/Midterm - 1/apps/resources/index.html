Suggested priority

Start with validation/leakage, the two SVM penalty conventions, ARIMA versus moving averages, logistic odds versus probability, and confusion-metric denominators. Then review CUSUM settings, seasonal indexing, PCA versus predictive relevance, and regression inference. This is a study recommendation based on the distinctions in the supplied material, not an exam-weight prediction.

Model purpose and interpretation

Before choosing a method, name the response, available predictors, intended decision, and deployment population. Separate explanation of past observations, prediction of future outcomes, and optimization of actions.

  • ISLR author companion courses

Scaling, units, and distance

Work through z=(x-mean)/SD and min-max scaling. Explain why changing units changes an unscaled distance. Learn transformation parameters on training data, then reuse them. Distinguish feature standardization from making each observation a unit-length vector.

  • scikit-learn preprocessing reference

SVM margins, hinge loss, and penalty conventions

Sketch f=0 and f=±1, label width 2/norm(w), and calculate hinge loss for margins above one, between zero and one, and below zero. Compare the placement of C and lambda in the stated objectives before predicting their effects.

  • StatQuest SVM video
  • ISLR classification lectures

Nearest neighbors and the meaning of k

Compare classification voting with regression averaging. Specify k, distance, scaling, weights, and tie handling. A larger neighborhood smooths the prediction but can hide local patterns; choose settings using held-out performance.

  • StatQuest video catalog: nearest neighbors
  • ISLR statistical-learning lectures

Cross-validation, leakage, and selection optimism

Draw the outer test holdout and inner CV folds. Repeat learned preprocessing inside each training fold. After selecting settings, refit on the non-test data and evaluate once on the untouched test set. Group or order observations when deployment requires it.

  • StatQuest cross-validation video
  • ISLR resampling lectures

K-means, distance geometry, and elbow selection

Sketch assign-to-nearest-center and recompute-means steps. For standard k-means, centers minimize squared Euclidean distance within assigned groups. WSS rewards additional clusters, so minimum WSS alone cannot determine a useful k. Compare geometry to the actual travel or service objective.

  • StatQuest k-means video
  • ISLR unsupervised-learning lectures

Outliers, context, and legitimate rare events

Ask whether a flagged point is an error, an unusual but relevant case, a context-specific anomaly, or part of an unusual sequence. Exclusion needs a reason connected to the intended population and task. Compare the consequences of preserving and excluding the observation.

  • ISLR linear-model diagnostics and unsupervised-learning lectures

CUSUM direction, allowance, and alarm threshold

Trace Splus=max(0,Splus_previous+x-mu-C). C is an allowance subtracted from each increment; T is a separate alarm threshold. A lower monitor reverses the deviation. An alarm signals a change under the chosen baseline and settings; it does not establish a cause.

  • NIST CUSUM explanation

Single exponential smoothing and flat forecasts

Calculate a level update and expand two steps to see declining historical weights. Alpha governs the response to new data. Without trend or seasonality, future point forecasts all equal the latest level; do not keep updating with unknown future observations.

  • Forecasting textbook: single smoothing

Holt level, trend, and forecast horizon

Keep level and trend as different quantities. Update level using the prior projected level and update trend using the level change. An h-step ordinary Holt forecast is level+h*trend. Trend units are response units per observation interval.

  • Forecasting textbook: trend smoothing

Holt-Winters seasonality and indexing

Use the observation index to define the seasonal period: a manufacturing batch can supply the cycle even if its calendar duration varies. Multiplicative factors are ratios around one; additive effects are amounts around zero. Match the forecast to the correct future seasonal phase.

  • Forecasting textbook: seasonal smoothing

ARIMA: AR lags, differencing, and MA innovations

Read p,d,q in order. AR terms use previous values of the modeled series; d specifies differencing; MA terms use previous innovations. This MA component is different from averaging recent observed values. Check whether the differenced series has an adequately stable mean and dependence structure.

  • Forecasting textbook: ARIMA
  • Forecasting textbook: stationarity and differencing

GARCH models variance, not the mean response

Separate the conditional mean from conditional variance. In a GARCH(1,1) update, both the previous squared shock and previous variance matter. Squaring removes shock direction. A variance forecast can stay large after a small shock when past variance is large.

  • arch project: mean and volatility model components

Forecast validation without future information

Use training windows that precede their forecast targets. Distinguish forecast errors from in-sample residuals. A rolling origin evaluates forecasts at repeated historical cutoffs, using only information available at each cutoff.

  • Forecasting textbook: time-series validation

Linear regression, coefficient interpretation, and interactions

Linear refers to coefficients, so powers and interactions of predictors can appear in a linear model. Hold other predictors fixed when interpreting an additive coefficient. With interactions, differentiate or compare complete predictions because the effect depends on the interacting predictor.

  • ISLR linear-regression lectures
  • StatQuest video catalog: multiple regression

P-values, effect sizes, confidence, and prediction

Interpret a p-value as a tail probability under the null model, not the probability the null is true. Distinguish statistical evidence from practical effect size and causality. A confidence interval for the mean response and a prediction interval for a new individual answer different questions.

  • StatQuest p-value video
  • ISLR regression-inference lectures

AIC, BIC, AICc, and likelihood

Use comparable responses, likelihood conventions, and parameter counts. Lower AIC/BIC is preferred. AIC=-2*logL+2*K; BIC=-2*logL+K*log(n). The AICc correction grows when n is small relative to K. A lower criterion does not certify causal interpretation or perfect prediction.

  • ISLR model-selection lectures

Residual plots and regression assumptions

Separate nonlinearity, unequal residual spread, dependence, and unusually influential observations. A curved pattern suggests a mean-model problem; changing spread suggests a variance problem. Assumptions concern errors conditional on predictors rather than requiring every predictor to be normally distributed.

  • ISLR regression diagnostics lectures

PCA: scores, loadings, and outcome relevance

Draw a rotated coordinate system. Scores are projections; loadings describe directions. Components maximize predictor variance subject to orthogonality, without consulting y. Retaining all components preserves the centered feature information; truncating them may discard predictive signal.

  • StatQuest PCA video
  • ISLR unsupervised-learning lectures

Box-Cox, detrending, and returning to original units

For positive y, use (y^lambda-1)/lambda, with log(y) as the zero-power limit. A transformation can help a model without guaranteeing all assumptions. Re-add a removed trend after predicting detrended values. Nonlinear inverse transformations need care when interpreting conditional means.

  • NIST Box-Cox explanation
  • ISLR regression lectures

Logistic probabilities versus odds

Convert eta to p=1/(1+exp(-eta)). Odds=p/(1-p); a one-unit predictor change multiplies odds by exp(beta) in an additive logistic model. It does not multiply probability by the same factor. A probability model and a classification threshold are separate choices.

  • StatQuest logistic-regression video
  • ISLR classification lectures

Confusion metrics, prevalence, and ROC

Define the positive event before writing TP,FP,FN,TN. Recall conditions on actual positives, precision on predicted positives, and specificity on actual negatives. ROC plots TPR versus FPR across thresholds. AUC measures ranking; it does not prescribe a threshold or guarantee probability calibration.

  • StatQuest ROC video
  • StatQuest video catalog: confusion metrics

Decision costs and the probability threshold

Compare expected losses for the two actions. With calibrated probability p, false-positive cost a and false-negative cost b, classify positive when a*(1-p)<b*p, equivalently p>a/(a+b). This relies on correct decisions having zero cost; more general costs change the calculation.

  • ISLR classification and decision-rule lectures

Random forests, bootstrap samples, and feature subsampling

Separate sampling observations with replacement from randomly limiting candidate features at each split. The second step reduces similarity among trees. Classification uses aggregated votes or probabilities; regression averages numerical predictions. Importance describes predictive usefulness, not causal direction.

  • StatQuest random-forest video
  • ISLR tree-model lectures

Trees, leaf predictions, impurity, and pruning

Follow split rules in sequence. Ordinary squared-error regression CART predicts a leaf mean; a model tree can fit a regression inside a leaf. Classification impurity and regression SSE are different criteria. More leaves can improve training fit while hurting held-out performance.

  • ISLR tree-model lectures
  • StatQuest video catalog: classification and regression trees

Introduced regression families

Know the lecture-level distinctions: Poisson for count means, Bayesian updating of a parameter distribution, splines as joined polynomial pieces, and neighbor regression as local averaging. Use companion lectures to clarify a distinction rather than treating every advanced implementation detail as required scope.

  • ISLR regression, nonlinear-model, and Bayesian companion lectures
